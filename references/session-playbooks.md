# Session playbooks

## Start

Ask what problem the user hopes the book will help them think through. Then establish only the minimum orientation needed: the work's central problem, context, likely difficulty, and two or three concepts to notice. Ask for an initial prediction or position so later changes in understanding are visible. Do not front-load a full summary or spoil the argument.

End with a small reading unit and one observation question. For example: “先读到作者明确反驳的第一种观点；回来后不用复述全文，只说他认为对方错在什么前提。”

## Continue

First ask for closed-book reconstruction of the section's job in the argument. Based on the reply, choose one route:

- Accurate but shallow: ask for mechanism, evidence, or consequence.
- Partly correct: name what is sound, isolate one missing link, and give a hint.
- Vague/blank: reduce scope, offer two orienting cues without turning them into a multiple-choice quiz, explain briefly if needed, then ask for a new formulation.
- Strong: introduce a counterargument, boundary case, comparison, or modern transfer.

Build an argument skeleton as `question → claim → reasons → evidence → objections → limits`, not a list of highlights.

## Finish

Run a closed-book synthesis before showing notes:

1. Ask for the book's core question and one-sentence answer.
2. Ask for the minimum argument chain that supports it.
3. Test one objection or transfer case.
4. Compare against the initial prediction and identify what changed.

Then correct the synthesis, update the archive, and schedule reviews. Completion means a baseline model exists, not that every concept is mastered.

## Review

Select up to three due items. Ask one at a time without notes. Grade by evidence:

- `fail`: cannot retrieve or major misconception;
- `partial`: retrieves claim but cannot explain support or boundaries;
- `pass`: accurate explanation in own words;
- `transfer`: accurate explanation plus novel application, comparison, or counterexample.

After feedback, ask for a corrected answer only when the error is meaningful. Adjust the queue and stop before fatigue turns review into guessing.

## Direct explanation

When the user says they do not understand, provide a layered explanation: plain-language claim, concrete example, role in the book's argument, and one common misunderstanding. Then ask one compact check-for-understanding question.
